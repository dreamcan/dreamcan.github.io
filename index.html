Dream What I Can: Sparse Visual Planning for
Long-Horizon Robot Manipulation

Paper (arXiv:2610.24981)

The world is not made of words

We introduce DreamCan, a sparse visual planner that can generate a sequence of sparse keyframes for robot manipulation task planning. Rather than generating dense frames, DreamCan generates visual subgoals that balance reachability—what the robot can achieve—and task utility—what moves the task forward.

Video

Key Results

  • Sparse visual planning enables long-horizon planning. We achieve substantially higher planning success rates than the prior methods on Language-Table long-horizon tasks.
  • Sparse visual planning enables learning from suboptimal data. We demonstrate that DreamCan benefits from scaling suboptimal action-free video data on RoboMimic-MH Square, without additional policy training.
  • Sparse visual planning enables test-time search and language steering. We show that DreamCan benefits from increased test-time compute and can be steered with language on Language-Table tasks.
  • Sparse visual planning can effectively guide a goal-conditioned low-level policy. We show that our planner can guide a small goal-conditioned flow-matching policy and achieve comparable results with large VLA/WAMs on LIBERO-Long, with no gradient or shared features between the planner and policy.
  • Sparse visual planning is effective on real-world tasks. We show that our method can deal with different real-world tasks including cardboard box folding (contact-rich task), box packing (compositional generalization task), and make-a-line (long-horizon symbolic planning task).

Method

DreamCan first employs an unsupervised approach to discover sparse visual subgoal sequences from robot video datasets that balance subgoal reachability and utility, then fine-tunes a pretrained video generation model (WAN 2.2 TI2V-5B) to generate the full sequence of subgoals.

DreamCan first learns a Reachability Value Function from action-free videos to measure transition difficulty between two frames.

What does reachability measure?

  • An RL value. In an MDP, VRπ is the expected discounted return for reaching frame sj from frame si: −1 per step until arrival, then 0.
  • A score between 0 and 1. Normalizing this value gives reachability. Earlier arrival means a higher score; τij is the first hitting time.
Rπ(si,sj)=Eπ[γτij] =1+(1−γ)VRπ(si,sj)∈[0,1]

How is it learned?

  • Learn from video transitions. Fit VRθ using an action-free, goal-conditioned variant of Implicit Q-Learning (IQL), an offline RL method.
  • Favor higher returns. An upper-expectile loss emphasizes transitions that reach the conditioning frame more efficiently.
Training objective & sampling

For video transitions D, termination flag di and target head θ¯, the TD target and expectile loss are:

The upper expectile favors higher-return transitions. We use α=0.75, γ=0.95 and Polyak rate ρ=0.005, with VR(sj,sj)=0. Second frames are sampled equally from future frames with geometric offsets and other trajectories.

Balance three needs

A good subgoal sequence should balance feasible transitions with meaningful task progress.

  • Reachability R: favor transitions the robot can make.
  • Task utility U: reward progress toward the instruction.
  • Spacing Ω: discourage clustered, redundant keyframes.

From frames s0,…,sT, select K ordered keyframes to maximize:

The weights control feasibility, progress and spacing. For m remaining subgoals, the spacing penalty compares the next gap with a reference gap:

Optimize the whole sequence

A locally attractive frame may leave poor choices later. Dynamic programming scores each next frame together with its best continuation, reusing results for the remaining sequence.

Jm(i) is the best future score from frame i with m keyframes left:

J0(i)=ωUU(si)Jm(i)=maxj>i[ωRlogR(si,sj)−ωsΩm(i,j)+Jm−1(j)]

Recover the plan. Start at JK(0) and follow the maximizing next frame at each step to obtain all K keyframes.

  1. Align sparse targets

    Repeat each target four times to match the VAE’s temporal compression: one latent slot per subgoal, plus one for the observation.

Two ways of test-time search

  1. Whole-sequence best-of-N: generate N complete plans and keep the one with the highest task-progress score.
  2. Anchored search: keep the best subgoal prefix as clean latents and regenerate the remaining sequence.

Findings

  1. DreamCan can benefit from test-time search compute, especially from anchored search.
  2. Dense planning can also improve with extra compute, but its final task progress remains below DreamCan's initial result.
  3. Uniform subgoal planning cannot benefit from test-time compute.

FAQ

What is DreamCan?

DreamCan is a hierarchical robot manipulation system with a high-level sparse visual planner and an independently trained, goal-conditioned low-level policy. Given the current image observation and a language instruction, the planner predicts a short sequence of visual subgoals. The policy turns these subgoals into robot actions, and the planner is periodically called again using the latest observation for closed-loop replanning.

Why plan with sparse visual subgoals instead of language or dense videos?

Language plans can leave contact locations, geometric relationships, and intermediate object configurations ambiguous. Dense video plans are expensive to generate and can accumulate prediction errors or overconstrain the controller. Uniformly spaced keyframes can include redundant motion, regressions, or transitions that are difficult to execute. DreamCan selects sparse subgoals that balance reachability with useful task progress.

What does the reachability value function measure?

The normalized reachability score estimates how quickly and reliably one visual state can be reached from another. It is a discounted first-hitting-time value, rather than a success probability within a fixed horizon. The model uses action-free, goal-conditioned expectile learning with frozen RADIO visual features.

The learned score is a proxy for dataset-supported reachability, assuming a downstream controller whose capabilities are broadly compatible with the recorded behavior. It is not simply a penalty for elapsed video time: the paper evaluates demonstrations with detours and repeated states to distinguish the two.

How do the planner and low-level policy communicate?

Their interface is the generated subgoal images; the planner and policy do not share gradients or hidden representations. The low-level controller is a 0.1B-parameter goal-conditioned flow-matching Transformer. It uses current visual observations, proprioceptive state, the language instruction, and several future subgoals to produce an action chunk.

The 0.1B parameter count refers only to the low-level policy. The high-level planner is adapted from a 5B-parameter pretrained video model.

How do test-time search and language steering work?

The paper evaluates whole-sequence best-of-N search and anchored search. Anchored search samples candidate continuations, commits a selected prefix as clean latents, and regenerates the remaining subgoals. Candidate plans are evaluated with task-progress scores, and additional generator calls improve planning progress in the evaluated settings.

The planner is conditioned on language instructions. For test-time steering, the paper also uses a vision-language model to write instruction-specific task-progress functions that select plans matching the requested outcome. Examples include different line arrangements in Language-Table and preferred object placements during box packing.
